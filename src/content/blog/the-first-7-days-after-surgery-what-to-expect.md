Having surgery in a foreign country can feel overwhelming, especially in the days right after your procedure. Korea is known for skilled surgeons, modern hospitals, and attentive nursing care, but knowing what's "normal" during recovery can ease a lot of anxiety. Here's a friendly, day-by-day guide to help you understand what your first week might look like.

## Day 0–1: Right After Surgery

Right after surgery, you'll likely still feel groggy from anesthesia. It's completely normal to feel cold, nauseated, confused, or unusually thirsty as the medication wears off. Nurses will check your vital signs frequently during this period, so don't be alarmed by the regular blood pressure checks and temperature readings.

Pain is usually most noticeable in the first 24 hours. If you're in significant discomfort, always tell your nurse — pain control gets easier with communication.

## Day 2–3: Early Mobility

Doctors in Korea often encourage patients to start moving as early as possible, even just sitting up or taking a few steps with assistance. This isn't meant to be uncomfortable for the sake of it — early movement helps prevent blood clots, supports lung function, and speeds up overall healing.

Expect some swelling, bruising, or tightness around the incision site. Mild fever (under 38°C) in the first couple of days can also be a normal inflammatory response, though your medical team will monitor it closely.

This is usually when your diet progresses too — starting with water, moving to liquids, then soft foods, as tolerated. Pumpkin juice is commonly recommended after surgery — especially after cosmetic procedures — to help reduce swelling.

## Day 3–5: Settling Into a Routine

By now, many patients notice improving energy and reduced pain, though everyone heals at a different pace. Wound care becomes part of your daily routine; nurses will check and redress your incision, and they'll teach you how to care for it once you're discharged.

If you're traveling to Korea for surgery, many larger clinics provide interpreter services for international patients. You can also work with a medical agency that offers language support throughout your treatment, including explaining medications, postoperative instructions, and discharge planning. If language is a barrier, don't hesitate to ask for assistance to ensure you fully understand your care.

## Day 5–7: Final Follow-up Appointment / Stitch Removal

Toward the end of the first week, international patients typically return to the clinic for their final follow-up appointment and stitch removal before flying home. Before discharge, you should receive:

- Clear wound care instructions
- A list of medications and dosing schedules
- Activity restrictions (no heavy lifting, bathing instructions, avoiding the sauna for 1 month)
- Warning signs that mean you should seek immediate care
- A follow-up appointment date

## Warning Signs to Watch For

Contact your clinic promptly if you notice any of the following:

- Fever above 38.5°C
- Increasing redness, warmth, or discharge from the incision
- Severe or worsening pain not controlled by medication
- Shortness of breath or chest pain

## A Few Tips for International Patients

Recovering from surgery away from home adds an extra layer of stress, so a little preparation goes a long way. It can help to keep your clinic's contact information handy and save your interpreter's number on your phone.

Most importantly, be patient with yourself. The first week is about gentle progress, not perfection — every body heals on its own timeline.
